Registering a death and getting the death certificate

It can feel like a lot to take in. You don't need to do any of this before the funeral, and we will walk you through each step when you are ready.

Who usually does the death certificate formalities?

In most families, a close relative registers the death: usually the husband, wife or partner, or a son or daughter. It is often done in the weeks after the funeral, once things have settled a little. Irish law calls this person the qualified informant.

The work is shared between three people:

The doctor

Confirms the death and completes the Medical Certificate of the Cause of Death. In a hospital, hospice or nursing home, the staff look after this.

Your family

One relative meets the registrar, gives the details and signs the register. After that, the family can order death certificates.

Us, your funeral director

We guide you through it all: we speak to the doctor, hospital or coroner, check the paperwork, and tell you which office to contact and what to bring.

Who can register a death

A relative who knows the details is asked first. If there is no relative available, someone else who knows the details can register, for example:

  • a person who was present at the death
  • a person who found the body or took charge of it
  • the person in charge of the hospital, nursing home or other building where the death happened
  • any other person who knows the required details

Step by step

  1. The doctor certifies the cause of death. Usually this is the doctor who cared for the person in the month before they died. If a doctor hadn't seen them recently, or the death was sudden or unexpected, the coroner is told instead (see below).
  2. Contact a civil registration office. You can register at any civil registration office in Ireland, not only the one nearest to where the person died. Most offices work by appointment. We will give you the details for the office in {{countyFull}}.
  3. Register within 3 months. The law asks for the death to be registered within three months. The funeral itself does not have to wait for this.
  4. Order death certificates. Once the death is registered, you can buy certified copies from the registrar or the General Register Office. A fee applies to each copy.

What to bring to the registrar

  • The Medical Certificate of the Cause of Death, if the doctor gave it to you (often the doctor sends it to the registrar directly)
  • Your own photo ID
  • Their PPS number, date and place of birth, and last address
  • Their occupation and civil status (married, widowed, single and so on), with the name of a spouse or civil partner
  • Their parents' names, including their mother's maiden name

If you don't have every detail, register anyway and tell the registrar what is missing. Please don't let a missing number add to your worry.

When the coroner is involved

A doctor must tell the coroner when a death was sudden, unexpected or unexplained, followed an accident, or when no doctor had seen the person recently. This is routine, and it does not mean anyone has done anything wrong.

  • The coroner may ask for a post-mortem examination. In most cases the funeral can still go ahead within days.
  • If the cause of death is natural, the coroner sends a certificate to the registrar and you register the death as usual.
  • If there is to be an inquest, the death is registered after it. Until then, the coroner's office can give the family an interim certificate of the fact of death. Banks, insurers and the Department of Social Protection usually accept it.

We deal with the coroner's office and the Gardaí for you, and we will keep you updated at each step.

How many death certificates will you need?

Families often need three or four: for the bank or credit union, pensions and the Department of Social Protection, insurance and the solicitor handling probate. Ask each one whether a photocopy will do; many organisations will look at the original and hand it back.

This guide is general information about civil registration in Ireland, not legal advice. Rules and fees change, so check the latest details with Citizens Information or your local civil registration office, or simply ask us.
